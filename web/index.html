Did the campaign sell?

Sales went up during the campaign. This walks through how I check how much of that the campaign caused, where it worked and how sure we can be. The data is simulated, so at the end we can compare the estimate with the true effect.

1 · The setup

Half the cities got the campaign, half didn't

160 stores in 16 cities. The campaign ran for 4 weeks in 8 cities picked at random (80 stores); the other 8 cities are the control group. Picking cities rather than stores keeps the campaign from spilling into control stores nearby.

2 · Is the lift real?

Campaign stores moved, control stores didn't

For each store I compare average daily sales during the campaign with the 12 weeks before, and subtract the typical change in control stores. That removes what happened to the whole chain anyway (season, weekdays, a general trend). Per-store lift is skewed, so I use rank tests that don't assume a normal distribution.

3 · Where it came from

One store in nine carried more than 40% of the effect

K-means groups campaign stores by their lift and their size. The point is practical: when a large share of the effect comes from a few stores, the next budget should look at what those stores have in common.

4 · Does it cluster?

The effect has a geography

Moran's I measures whether stores with high lift sit next to other stores with high lift (each store is compared with its six nearest neighbors). LISA does the same store by store and marks the ones that are part of a significant cluster, using 999 random reshuffles as the baseline.

5 · How much?

What sales would have been without the campaign

Before the campaign, total daily sales in campaign cities track control cities closely. I fit that relationship on the 12 weeks before, then use control sales during the campaign to predict what campaign cities would have sold without it. The gap is the effect; the interval comes from resampling the pre-campaign errors. This is the idea behind Causal Impact, written as a plain regression.
